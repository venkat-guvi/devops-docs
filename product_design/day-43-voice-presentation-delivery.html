Part 1: Introduction to Presentation Skills

Most designers focus almost entirely on what they present — the wireframes, the research, the flows. But how you present is equally powerful. This section challenges that assumption and reframes presentation as a core design skill.

Why Delivery Matters as Much as Content

Imagine two designers presenting the same solution. One mumbles through slides, reads off the screen, and rushes through the conclusion. The other speaks clearly, pauses for impact, and connects each decision to the user's need. The second designer gets hired — not because their work is better, but because their story landed.

Part 2: Structuring Your Verbal Presentation

A presentation without structure is like a user flow without logic. Your audience gets lost, and they stop trusting you. Every great presentation follows a simple three-part arc.

Opening, Body, Closing

OPENING (First 60 seconds)Hook your audience immediately. Open with a question, a surprising stat, or a bold statement. State what you're covering and why it matters to them. Don't start with "Hi, so umm today I'll be talking about..."CLOSING (Last 60 seconds)Summarise 3 key takeaways. End with a clear statement of impact. Leave the audience with something to think about. Never end with "That's it, yeah, thanks." End with intention.

The Body — Maintaining Flow

The body is where most presentations fall apart. Without transitions, every slide feels disconnected. Use verbal signposting to guide your audience:

"Before I get into the solution, let me show you what was broken..."

"Now that you understand the problem, here's how we approached it..."

"This connects directly to what I showed earlier about user behaviour..."

"And finally, here's what we validated with real users..."

Verbal Transitions Cheat Sheet

MoveExample phrase
Problem → Research"To understand why this was happening, we went to talk to users..."
Research → Insight"What surprised us most was..."
Insight → Design"That insight directly led us to this design decision..."
Design → Outcome"When we tested this with 8 users, here's what we found..."
Outcome → Takeaway"The key learning here is..."

Part 4 : Confidence Building

Nervousness is not the enemy — unmanaged nervousness is. Every experienced presenter gets nervous. The difference is that they've learned to channel it.

Handling Nervousness

Prepare until you know your material, not just your slides

Breathe intentionally — 4 counts in, 4 counts out before you begin

Accept the nerves — "I'm excited" and "I'm nervous" produce the same physiological response. Label it as excitement.

Have your first two sentences memorized — once you're moving, momentum carries you

Arrive early, test your setup, stand in the room — familiarity reduces anxiety

Speaking with Clarity Under Pressure

When you're nervous, your brain defaults to rambling. Combat this with structure: every question or point gets the PEE structure.

PPointState your main point in 1 sentence
EEvidence / ExampleBack it up with a specific example, data point, or user quote
EExplain / ImpactConnect it back — what does this mean for the user or the design outcome?

Part 5 : Engaging Your Audience

A presentation is not a monologue — it's a conversation with a room. Even in a formal panel setting, the most memorable presenters make every person feel like they're being spoken to directly.

Eye Contact (in a Panel Setting)

You can't maintain literal eye contact with every person when presenting online or to a panel — but you can create the perception of it.

In-person: Move your gaze slowly across the room. Land on one person for 3–5 seconds per statement.

Virtual: Look into the camera lens, not at your own video thumbnail.

Panels: Address specific panelists by name when relevant — "As you know from a UX research background..."

Avoid staring at your slides — trust your content, speak to your audience

Pausing & Emphasis

Pause and emphasis are punctuation for the spoken word. They tell your audience what to pay attention to.

How to Emphasise a Word or Phrase

Slow down slightly before the key word

Increase volume slightly on the emphasized word

Use a downward pitch at the end of the sentence to signal a conclusion

Try it: "We didn't just redesign the interface. We redesigned the experience."

Part 7 : Handling Q&A Sessions

The Q&A is where confidence is tested. Many designers dread it — but the Q&A is actually your best opportunity to demonstrate depth of thinking and professionalism.

Listening Carefully

Most presenters start forming their answer before the question is finished. This creates rushed, incomplete responses. Practice active listening:

Let the question finish completely before you begin formulating an answer

If the question is long or complex, paraphrase it back: "So if I understand correctly, you're asking about how we validated this with users?"

It's okay to pause for 3–5 seconds before answering — it signals that you're thinking, not guessing

Answering Clearly and Confidently

Lead with the answer, then explain — not the other way around

Use "That's a great question" sparingly — it delays your answer and can sound rehearsed

If you don't know: "That's not something we had the chance to test in this round, but here's how I'd approach it..."

If challenged: Don't defend — explore. "Walk me through what you're seeing there — I want to make sure I'm addressing your concern directly."

Part 8 : Common Mistakes

These are the patterns that consistently undermine otherwise strong presentations. Review them, recognize them in your own habits, and consciously eliminate them.

MistakeWhat it Looks LikeFix
Reading SlidesPresenter turns to screen, reads bullet by bullet, back to audienceSlides are speaker notes for the audience — you should know your content, not read it
RushingPresenter races through slides, skips details, finishes early but with no impactPractice with a timer. Slow is professional. Fast is nervous.
No StructurePresentation feels like a list of features, not a story. No arc, no logic, no flow.Use the Problem → Insight → Decision → Outcome arc every time
Apology Opening"Sorry, I'm a bit nervous" or "This isn't quite finished but..."Never apologise at the start. Start strong. Confidence is a choice.
Ignoring the PanelOnly looking at slides or notes, no eye contact with evaluatorsSpeak to the people, not the screen. The panel is your audience.
No Clear TakeawayPresentation ends with "yeah so that's basically it, any questions?"End with 3 clear takeaways and a strong closing statement of impact

Part 9 : Mock Presentation Scenarios

Writing for a Reader, Not for Yourself

Most designers write case studies as if they are explaining their own memory of a project. The problem is, the reader was not there. They have no context, no emotional investment, and very limited attention.

Great case study writing assumes the reader knows nothing and cares even less — and earns their attention sentence by sentence.

The Three Cs of Good Case Study Writing

🎯 ClearEvery sentence should have one job: move the reader forward. Eliminate jargon, hedging, and filler phrases like 'in order to' or 'as we can see.'🔗 CoherentEvery section should connect to the one before it. The reader should never feel lost or wonder 'why are they telling me this?'✨ CompellingUse specific, concrete details. Not 'users were frustrated' but 'users sent 47 support tickets in two weeks about the same broken flow.'

Writing With Specificity

The single biggest writing upgrade a designer can make is replacing vague statements with specific ones.

❌ WeakWe did user research and found some pain points.
✅ StrongWe interviewed 8 users over 5 days and discovered a consistent pattern: 6 out of 8 couldn’t complete checkout without abandoning the cart at least once.
❌ WeakThe design improved the experience significantly.
✅ StrongAfter launch, the onboarding completion rate increased from 38% to 71% over six weeks, and support tickets about account setup dropped by 55%.
