Part 2: Synthesizing Research Data

What Is Synthesis?

Synthesis is the process of making sense of everything you collected during research. It's where the real design thinking begins.

Why Synthesis Matters

Most beginner designers skip synthesis and jump straight to solutions. This is the most dangerous mistake in UX. Synthesis is not optional — it is the bridge between understanding and creating.

Without Synthesis

You design based on assumptions

You solve the wrong problems

Users reject the final product

Your decisions are hard to justify

With Synthesis

You design based on evidence

You solve the right problems first

Users feel understood and valued

Your decisions are backed by data

Turning Raw Data Into Insights

Raw data is observations, quotes, and facts. Insights are the meaning behind them — the 'so what.'

🔴 Raw Data (What they said)

"I always forget my password and have to reset it every week"

🟢 Insight (What it means)

Users struggle with authentication friction. The login experience is a major drop-off risk and creates frustration.

"I don't know where to find my past orders"

Navigation lacks discoverability. Users cannot locate key features without external help.

Part 3 : Affinity Mapping (Conceptual)

What Is Affinity Mapping?

Affinity Mapping (also called Affinity Diagramming) is a collaborative technique to organize large amounts of qualitative data into meaningful groups based on their natural relationships.

How Affinity Mapping Works

The process follows these steps:

Write each observation, quote, or data point on a separate sticky note

Place all notes on a board without any organization

Look for natural clusters — notes that feel related

Group related notes together

Name each group with a theme (this becomes an insight category)

Look for patterns and tensions across groups

Grouping Patterns and Behaviors

When grouping notes, look for:

Patterns (Repeating Themes)

Multiple users facing the same issue

Consistent behaviors or habits

Repeated frustrations or delights

Similar mental models across users

Behaviors (What users actually do)

Workarounds users create

Steps users skip or struggle with

Moments of confusion or hesitation

When users abandon a task

Identifying Key Themes

After grouping, name your themes — these become the pillars of your design problem. Good themes are:

Descriptive — they describe the user experience, not the feature

Insight-driven — they reflect a finding, not just a label

Actionable — they point you toward design opportunities

Part 4 : Defining User Needs

What Are User Needs?

After synthesis, you have themes. Now you need to convert those themes into clearly stated user needs. A user need is not a feature request — it is a human need that a design solution must address.

The Three Lenses: Pain Points, Motivations, Goals

After synthesis, you have themes. Now you need to convert those themes into clearly stated user needs. A user need is not a feature request — it is a human need that a design solution must address.

😣 Pain Points

What frustrates, blocks, or slows users down in their current experience.

Example: 'I waste 10 minutes every day searching for the file I need'

💡 Motivations

What drives users — the emotional or practical reason behind their actions.

Example: 'I want to feel in control of my work'

🎯 Goals

The outcome the user wants to achieve — the definition of success for them.

Example: 'Complete my project report by end of day without switching tools'

Part 5 : How Might We Statements

Converting Problems Into Opportunities

How Might We (HMW) statements are a design thinking tool that reframes problems as opportunities for design. They are short, open-ended questions that invite creative solutions without prescribing them.

The words 'How Might We' are intentional: 'How' assumes a solution exists. 'Might' means there's no single correct answer. 'We' makes it collaborative.

From Problem to HMW

Problem / Insight

How Might We Statement

Users forget their password constantly

HMW make authentication so easy that users never feel locked out?

Users can't find past orders

HMW help users rediscover their history in a way that feels natural?

New users feel overwhelmed by features

HMW welcome new users so they feel confident from their very first session?

Tips for Writing Good HMW Statements

Not too broad: 'HMW improve the app' → too vague

Not too narrow: 'HMW add a fingerprint login' → too prescriptive

Just right: specific enough to give direction, open enough to allow creativity

One HMW per problem — don't combine multiple problems into one

Part 6 : Solution Ideation

What Is Ideation in Product Design?

Ideation is the creative phase of design thinking where you generate a wide range of possible solutions to your defined problem. It's not about finding the right answer yet — it's about exploring the full possibility space.

In ideation, quantity beats quality — at first. The goal is to surface as many ideas as possible, including wild and impractical ones, because great ideas often emerge from unexpected combinations.

Divergent vs. Convergent Thinking

The ideation process moves through two distinct modes of thinking:

Divergent Thinking (Open Up)

Generate as many ideas as possible

Suspend judgment completely

Build on others' ideas (yes, and...)

Wild and impractical ideas are welcome

No filtering, no criticism

Convergent Thinking (Narrow Down)

Evaluate and filter generated ideas

Apply logic and feasibility

Group similar ideas together

Rank based on criteria

Select the most promising directions

Part 7 : Idea Generation Techniques

Technique 1: Brainstorming

Brainstorming is the most widely used ideation method. When done correctly, it's surprisingly powerful. When done poorly, it produces group think and mediocre ideas.

Rules of Good Brainstorming

Defer judgment — no criticism during generation

Encourage wild ideas — aim for the extreme

Build on ideas — 'yes, and...' thinking

Stay focused on the HMW question

Time-box it — 5-8 minutes per round

Common Mistakes

Evaluating while generating

One person dominating the session

Not enough psychological safety to share bold ideas

Starting with constraints instead of possibilities

Stopping at the first good idea

Lens 2: User Value vs. Business Value

A solution must serve both the user and the business. Use this second lens to ensure balance:

User Value Questions

Does this solve a real user pain point?

Does it reduce friction or create delight?

Would users notice if this feature disappeared?

Does it align with what users said they need?

Business Value Questions

Does this support business goals (growth, retention, revenue)?

Is it achievable within current team capacity?

Does it differentiate the product?

Can the impact be measured?

Part 11 : Research → Insights → Ideas

Closing the Loop

The entire session today has been about one journey: taking what you observed in the field, making sense of it through synthesis, and channeling it into creative, grounded design solutions.

The Designer's Mindset

The best UX designers hold two qualities simultaneously:

Rigor — they trust their research, follow the process, and build on evidence

Creativity — they push past obvious solutions into genuinely novel territory

💡 Key Takeaways

Research without synthesis is just data. Synthesis without ideation is just analysis. Ideation without research is just guessing. Together, they are design thinking.
