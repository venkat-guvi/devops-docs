Part 1: Introduction to UX Research

1.1 What is UX Research?

UX Research is the systematic study of users and their needs, behaviors, motivations, and mental models in relation to a product or service. It is the practice of collecting evidence to reduce uncertainty before making design decisions.

Without research, design is guesswork. With research, design becomes a response to real human needs.

SLIDE: What is UX Research?

UX Research = systematic study of users to inform design decisions

It answers: Who are our users? What do they need? How do they think?

Research reduces assumptions - it replaces "I think users want X" with "Users told us X"

It happens before, during, and after the design process - not just at the start

Good research is the difference between building the right product and building it right

Quote to remember: "You are not the user." - Every UX researcher ever

1.2 Why UX Research Matters

The cost of fixing a problem grows exponentially the later it is discovered. A problem found in research costs almost nothing to fix. A problem found after launch can cost a company its users, its reputation, and its revenue.

The Cost of Skipping Research

Design stage: A wrong assumption costs 1 hour to fix in a wireframe

Development stage: The same wrong assumption costs 10 hours to fix in code

Post-launch stage: The same assumption costs 100+ hours to fix and re-release

Research is the cheapest insurance policy a product team can buy

Real example: 70% of product features go unused - often because teams built what they assumed users wanted, not what users actually needed

1.3 Who Does UX Research?

UX Research is not exclusively the job of a dedicated researcher. In many teams - especially in startups and small agencies - designers, product managers, and developers conduct research. Design literacy in research is essential for everyone on the product team.

Dedicated UX Researcher

Designer Doing Research

Full-time role focused on research methodology

Conducts research alongside design work

Deep expertise in statistical analysis

Focuses on quick, lightweight methods

Manages participant recruitment at scale

Smaller participant pools - 5 to 8 users

Owns the research roadmap

Research feeds directly into their own designs

Works across multiple product teams

Most common in startups and agencies

1.4 Research vs Intuition

Experienced designers develop strong intuition. But intuition is shaped by experience - and your experience is not your user. Research validates or challenges intuition. The best teams use both: intuition to form hypotheses, research to test them.

SLIDE: Research vs Intuition

Intuition says: Users will find this button easily

Research says: 6 out of 8 users missed the button entirely

Intuition is a starting point - research is the evidence

Never kill an idea without research. Never ship a feature without research.

Research does not replace creativity - it channels it toward real problems

WORKSHOP ACTIVITY: The Assumption Audit

Each participant takes a product they use daily (an app, a website, a service).

Write 5 things you assume the product designers know about their users.

For each assumption, ask: How could you test this? What method would you use?

Share one assumption with the group. Discuss: Is this assumption provable? What research would give you confidence?

Debrief: most assumptions can be tested. The question is whether teams choose to test them.

Part 2: Role of Research in Product Design

2.1 Research as a Design Compass

Research does not tell you what to design. It tells you what problem to solve, for whom, and in what context. The designer still brings creativity, craft, and judgment. Research simply ensures that creativity is applied to the right problem.

SLIDE: Research as a Compass

Research answers: What problem exists? Who has it? How severe is it?

Design answers: What solution could solve this problem?

Research without design = data without action

Design without research = solutions without problems

Together they form a feedback loop, not a one-time handoff

2.2 Where Research Lives in the Product Design Process

Research is not a one-time activity at the start of a project. It runs across the entire product lifecycle - from discovery through to post-launch measurement.

Research at Each Stage

Discover: Generative research - understand who the users are and what problems exist. Methods: interviews, field studies, diary studies.

Define: Synthesis - translate raw research into insights, personas, and problem statements.

Ideate: Concept testing - early feedback on ideas before investing in design.

Design: Usability testing - test wireframes and prototypes with users.

Test: Evaluative research - is the design working? What needs iteration?

Launch & Measure: Analytics, surveys, and ongoing user feedback to track real-world performance.

2.3 Research Reduces Risk

Every product decision carries risk. Research is a risk management tool. The more uncertain a decision, the more valuable research becomes. The goal is not to eliminate all risk but to make informed decisions with the best available evidence.

How Research Reduces Product Risk

Validates that the problem is real and worth solving before investment

Identifies the right user segment before building features for the wrong audience

Prevents building features users do not need or will not use

Surfaces usability problems before they reach customers

Provides evidence to resolve team debates and stakeholder disagreements

Creates a feedback loop so the product improves over time, not just at launch

Part 3: Types of UX Research - Generative vs Evaluative

Understanding the two fundamental types of UX research is the foundation of knowing which method to choose and when. Every research activity falls into one of these two categories.

3.1 Generative Research

Generative research is exploratory. It is conducted when you do not yet fully understand the user or the problem space. The goal is to generate insights, not to test a specific design or solution.

Generative Research - Key Characteristics

Also called: Exploratory research, Discovery research, Foundational research

Primary question: What is the problem? Who has it? What is their context?

Conducted at the beginning of a project - before solutions are defined

Output: Insights, themes, opportunities, personas, journey maps, problem statements

Typical methods: User interviews, ethnographic field studies, diary studies, contextual inquiry

Sample size: Typically 5 to 15 participants - quality of insight over quantity

Nature of data: Primarily qualitative - rich, nuanced, narrative

3.2 Evaluative Research

Evaluative research is assessment-focused. It is conducted when you have a design, concept, or prototype and want to know whether it works for users. The goal is to evaluate, not to discover.

Evaluative Research - Key Characteristics

Also called: Usability research, Validation research, Testing research

Primary question: Does this solution work? Can users complete key tasks?

Conducted during and after design - when something exists to test

Output: Usability findings, task success rates, error rates, iteration recommendations

Typical methods: Usability testing, A/B testing, surveys, heuristic evaluation

Sample size: 5 participants for qualitative usability; larger samples for quantitative

Nature of data: Can be qualitative and quantitative

3.3 Side-by-Side Comparison

3.4 Attitudinal vs Behavioral Research

A secondary distinction in research type: attitudinal research captures what users say, while behavioral research captures what users do. The gap between the two is often where the most important insights hide.

SLIDE: Attitudinal vs Behavioral

Attitudinal: What users say they do, think, or feel - surveys, interviews, focus groups

Behavioral: What users actually do - analytics, eye-tracking, usability testing, session recordings

The classic gap: "I would definitely use this feature" (attitudinal) vs. 0% adoption rate (behavioral)

Best practice: triangulate both - what users say plus what they do gives the fullest picture

Example: A user says the checkout is "fine" but abandons it at the same step every time

3.5 Qualitative vs Quantitative Research

Qualitative Research

Quantitative Research

Explores the why and how

Measures the what and how many

Small sample - 5 to 15 users

Large sample - 100+ users

Interviews, observations, diary studies

Surveys, analytics, A/B tests

Rich, nuanced, narrative data

Numerical, measurable data

Uncovers unexpected themes

Confirms patterns at scale

Cannot be generalized statistically

Needs qualitative to explain the why

Part 4: Primary Research - Interviews & Field Study Basics

Primary research means collecting data directly from users - you are the one gathering the evidence, not relying on someone else who gathered it before you. It is the closest you can get to your users without actually being them.

4.1 What is Primary Research?

Primary Research - Definition & Core Idea

Data collected firsthand, directly from users or the field

You design the study, recruit participants, conduct the sessions, and analyze the results

It is original - it did not exist before you collected it

More time-intensive than secondary research, but directly relevant to your product and users

Core methods in UX: User interviews, contextual inquiry, usability testing, diary studies, field observation

Primary research answers questions that no existing data source can answer for you

4.2 User Interviews

The user interview is the most widely used primary research method in UX. A well-conducted interview can surface needs, behaviors, mental models, and pain points that no analytics dashboard will ever reveal.

4.2.1 Interview Types

Type

When to Use

Structure Level

Structured Interview

When you need comparable data across many participants - like a survey, but verbal

High - same questions, same order for all participants

Semi-Structured Interview

Most common in UX - a guide with flexibility to follow interesting threads

Medium - core questions fixed, probes flexible

Unstructured Interview

Deep exploratory conversations - early discovery with very little known

Low - conversational, emerges from the participant

Contextual Interview

Conducted where the user works or lives - combines interview with observation

Medium to Low - guided by what happens in context

4.2.2 Writing an Interview Guide

An interview guide is not a script. It is a structured set of topic areas and questions that keep the session focused without constraining the conversation. A good guide ensures consistency while allowing discovery.

The Interview Guide - Structure

Section 1 - Warm Up (5 min): Build rapport. Ask about the participant role, daily routine, and general context. Never start with the core questions immediately.

Section 2 - Context Setting (5-10 min): Understand the domain. Ask them to walk you through a typical day or relevant task without mentioning your product.

Section 3 - Core Questions (20-30 min): Your primary research questions. Open-ended, behaviorally focused. Use probes to go deeper.

Section 4 - Exploration (10 min): Follow unexpected threads. Ask about edge cases, exceptions, and workarounds.

Section 5 - Wrap Up (5 min): Ask if there is anything else. Thank the participant. Leave space for them to share what they wished you had asked.

4.2.3 Question Types - Good vs Bad

Strong Interview Questions

Weak Interview Questions

Tell me about the last time you had to book a flight.

Would you use a feature that did X?

Walk me through how you decide which product to buy.

Do you like how this app is designed?

What is the most frustrating part of managing your finances?

Do you agree that the checkout process is too long?

How do you currently keep track of your tasks?

On a scale of 1 to 10, how satisfied are you?

Can you show me how you would do that?

Did you find the navigation easy to use?

The Five Core Probing Techniques

Silent probe: Say nothing. Silence makes participants uncomfortable - they will fill it with valuable information.

Echo probe: Repeat the last few words back as a question. "You said it felt confusing?" Encourages them to elaborate.

Clarification probe: "When you say X, what do you mean by that?" Removes ambiguity.

Laddering: "Why is that important to you?" - goes deeper from behavior to motivation to value.

Specific example probe: "Can you give me a specific example of when that happened?" Grounds the conversation in concrete reality.

4.3 Field Study Basics

A field study - also called ethnographic research or contextual inquiry - means going to where users actually are and observing them in their natural environment. It reveals what people do, not just what they say they do.

Why Field Studies Matter

Users often cannot accurately describe their own behavior - they do not remember, or they describe the ideal, not the real

Context shapes behavior: how someone uses a hospital system at 3 AM is very different from how they describe it in a research interview

Field studies surface environmental factors: distractions, tools, colleagues, time pressure - none of which appear in a lab

You will notice things users never think to mention because they are invisible to them - the workarounds, the hacks, the shortcuts

Classic insight from field research: Post-it notes covering monitors - discovered that users needed to remember too many passwords

Part 5: Secondary Research - Existing Data & Reports

Secondary research is research that already exists - data, reports, papers, and studies collected by others. It is faster and cheaper than primary research, and when used well, it can significantly sharpen the questions you ask in primary research.

5.1 What is Secondary Research?

Secondary Research - Definition & Role

Data or findings gathered by someone else, for a different purpose, before your project started

Also called: desk research, existing literature, prior art, background research

It does not replace primary research - it informs and focuses it

Use secondary research to: understand the domain, identify known pain points, size the market, review competitor behavior, and find gaps in existing knowledge

The core question: What is already known? What can we build on? What do we still need to discover?

5.2 Types of Secondary Research Sources

5.3 How to Evaluate Secondary Sources

Not all secondary sources are equally useful. The ability to critically evaluate a source is what separates a rigorous researcher from someone who confirms their biases with random data.

The CRAAP Test - Evaluating Sources

Currency: How recent is the data? Technology and behavior change fast - a 2015 study on mobile app usage may be irrelevant today.

Relevance: Does it apply to your specific users, market, and context? Global data may not reflect your local market.

Authority: Who produced it? What credentials or methodology do they have? Is the institution or author credible?

Accuracy: Can the claims be verified? Are there citations? Is the methodology described and sound?

Purpose: Why was it published? Is it a marketing report designed to sell a product? An independent study? Know the agenda behind the data.

5.4 App Store Reviews as Secondary Research

One of the most accessible and underused secondary research sources for UX designers is app store reviews. They are real, unfiltered user feedback at scale - and they are free.

Mining App Store Reviews

Read 1-star and 2-star reviews for your product and competitors - these are your most motivated critics

Look for repeated patterns: the same complaint in 20 reviews is a validated pain point

Look for the language users use - their exact words are often the best copy for forms, error messages, and onboarding

Read 5-star reviews too - they tell you what to protect when you redesign

Sort by recency AND by relevance - recent reviews reflect current state, top reviews reflect enduring themes

Tools: AppFollow, Appbot, Sensor Tower - all aggregate and categorize reviews at scale

5.5 Combining Primary and Secondary Research

The most effective research programs use primary and secondary research together. Secondary research shapes the questions you ask in primary research. Primary research validates, challenges, or adds nuance to secondary findings.

Start with Secondary Research

Then Conduct Primary Research

Build domain knowledge before talking to users

Validate whether secondary findings apply to your users

Identify known pain points and gaps

Uncover nuances that aggregate data misses

Scope your primary research questions

Capture context that reports cannot contain

Avoid asking users about what is already known

Identify what is unique about your users vs the general population

Set benchmarks to compare your findings against

Generate insights that no existing source can provide

6.2 Phase 1 - Plan

Planning is the most underrated phase of research. A well-planned study takes two to three times less time to execute than a poorly planned one. Planning decisions made at the start shape the quality of every downstream output.

Research Planning - Key Decisions

Research question: What is the one thing we most need to know? Be specific. "Understand users" is not a research question.

Method selection: Which method best answers this question given our time, budget, and access to users?

Participant criteria (screener): Who exactly are we recruiting? Age, behavior, experience, platform, context - all must be defined.

Sample size: How many participants? Rule of thumb - 5 for qualitative, 40+ for quantitative surveys.

Research guide / script: What will we ask or observe? How will the session be structured?

Logistics: Where? When? Remote or in-person? Recording consent? Incentives for participants?

6.3 Phase 2 - Collect

Data collection is the execution phase. The quality of your data depends on how well you follow your plan and how well you adapt when unexpected things happen in the session.

Data Collection Best Practices

Always record with consent - memory is unreliable, especially for nuance and exact wording

Take notes even if you are recording - write down your observations and emerging thoughts separately from transcript

Debrief with your co-researcher immediately after each session while it is fresh

Do not analyze during collection - stay in listening mode to avoid confirmation bias

Never lead the participant toward a conclusion - your job is to hear their experience, not confirm your hypothesis

Stop recruiting when you reach saturation - when new participants are not generating new themes

6.4 Phase 3 - Analyze

Analysis transforms raw data - transcripts, notes, recordings - into patterns. The most common analysis method in qualitative UX research is affinity diagramming, also called thematic analysis.

Affinity Diagramming - How It Works

Step 1: Write each observation or quote on a separate sticky note (one idea per note)

Step 2: Place all notes on a wall or whiteboard without structure - just get them visible

Step 3: Silently group similar notes together - resist labeling groups too early

Step 4: Label each group with a theme - the theme describes what all notes in the group share

Step 5: Look for relationships between groups - are any themes connected or in tension?

Step 6: Review outliers - notes that did not fit any group. Outliers sometimes contain the most important insights.

Digital tool: FigJam, Miro, Dovetail, or Notion work well for remote affinity diagramming

6.5 Phase 4 - Synthesize

Synthesis is the creative act of turning analyzed data into actionable insights. An insight is not an observation - it is an interpretation of what an observation means for design.

Observation (What you saw)

Insight (What it means)

5 out of 7 users did not notice the notifications bell icon

Insight drives design decisions

The notification system is not surfacing urgent information at the right time or in the right form

Recommendation: Move notifications to a persistent banner, not a hidden icon

Users took an average of 4 minutes to find the settings page

Insight drives design decisions

The information architecture does not match how users expect to find account controls

Recommendation: Restructure settings under a clearly labeled account menu in the main navigation

Insight vs Observation - The Key Distinction

Observation: "Users frequently opened the wrong menu item."

Analysis: "This happened 6 out of 8 sessions, always with the Settings vs Account menu."

Insight: "Users do not distinguish between Settings and Account - they treat them as the same concept."

Design implication (How Might We): How might we consolidate Settings and Account into a single, intuitively labeled destination?

The HMW (How Might We) question is the bridge from insight to ideation. One insight should generate several HMWs.

Part 7: Research Documentation & Deliverables

Duration: 15 minutes | Method: Lecture + Examples + Workshop

Research that is not documented and communicated effectively does not exist for the rest of the team. Documentation is not bureaucracy - it is the mechanism by which research creates lasting value across the organization.

7.1 Why Documentation Matters

The Value of Research Documentation

Preserves institutional memory - research findings outlast the researcher who collected them

Enables collaboration - other team members can build on documented research without starting over

Creates accountability - documented decisions can be traced back to evidence

Supports stakeholder communication - research reports translate evidence into business language

Informs future research - knowing what is already known prevents duplicate effort

Legal and ethical compliance - documentation of consent, data handling, and participant information

7.2 Core Research Deliverables

What It Contains

7.3 The Research Report

The research report is the primary communication artifact of a study. It translates evidence into action. A good report is not a data dump - it is a story that leads the reader from context to evidence to insight to recommendation.

Research Report - Recommended Structure

Section 1 - Executive Summary (1 page): The 3 to 5 most important findings, stated plainly. Busy stakeholders will read nothing else.

Section 2 - Background & Research Questions: Why was this study conducted? What questions did it seek to answer?

Section 3 - Methodology: Who did we talk to? How many? What method? How were participants recruited?

Section 4 - Key Findings: Organized by theme, not by participant. Each finding supported by at least one direct quote.

Section 5 - Insights & Implications: What do the findings mean for the product? Use the insight format: Observation + Interpretation + Implication.

Section 6 - Recommendations: Specific, actionable design directions. Prioritized by impact and feasibility.

Section 7 - Appendix: Full methodology, screener, discussion guide, raw notes (optional - for internal use only)

7.4 Persona - A Core Research Deliverable

A persona is a composite archetype derived from research patterns. It is NOT a fictional character invented by a designer. A research-based persona is built from direct evidence - patterns of behavior, goals, and frustrations observed across multiple participants.

Research-Based Persona

Assumption-Based Persona

Built from interview and observation data

Built from team assumptions or stereotypes

Behaviors and frustrations come from direct quotes

Behaviors are invented, not observed

Validated against multiple participants

No participant validation

Owned by the research, updated with new data

Reflects the team's mental model of users

Reflects actual user diversity and complexity

Can entrench bias and lead to wrong design decisions

Persona - Essential Components

Name and photo: A representative but clearly fictional identity (not a stock photo stereotype)

Role and context: What do they do? Where? With what tools and constraints?

Goals: What are they trying to achieve? Both practical and emotional goals.

Behaviors: What do they actually do - not what they say they do?

Frustrations and pain points: Where does the current experience fail them?

Motivations: What drives their decisions? What do they care about most?

Technology relationship: How comfortable are they with digital tools? What devices do they use?

Key quote: One direct quote from research that captures their voice and perspective
