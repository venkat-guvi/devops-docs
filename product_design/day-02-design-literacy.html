Part 1: Design Literacy — Good Design vs Bad Design

Design Literacy

Design literacy is the ability to recognize, understand, and critically evaluate how the human-made world—from physical products to websites—is designed, focusing on why things work (or fail) rather than just technical execution.

Design is not decoration — it is communication

Design literacy = the ability to READ and EVALUATE visual decisions

Applicable to everyone: designers, developers, product managers, clients

Good design is invisible. Bad design is loud.

What is Good Design?

Good design solves a problem. It is functional, accessible, intentional, and consistent. The user should never have to think about how to use it — it should feel effortless and natural.

📌

What is Bad Design?

Bad design creates friction. It confuses, overwhelms, misleads, or simply fails to communicate. Bad design isn't always ugly — sometimes it looks beautiful but fails to function. And sometimes it's purely aesthetic neglect.

Part 2: The Four Pillars of Good Design

The four pillars of good design, defined as functionality, reliability, usability, and aesthetics (FRUA), have the potential to provide a solid foundation for creating products or experiences that effectively meet user needs and expectations, especially when addressed in the correct order.

FunctionalityA functional design effectively solves the problem it was created for.Without functionality, a design can appear beautiful but be useless.The "Functionality" isn't just the map; it’s the massive invisible engine that connects GPS data, payment processing, and driver availability in real-time to get a car to your exact door.
ReliabilityReliability means that the product consistently performs as expected over time. It should be dependable and durable. If a design fails frequently or doesn’t perform as promised, it will frustrate users and erode trust.WhatsApp is the global standard for reliability because it delivers messages instantly with minimal data, rarely crashes, and shows you exactly when a message is sent, delivered, and read.
UsabilityUsability is about how easy and intuitive the product is to use. Even if a design is functional, if it’s hard to use, it won’t meet user needs.Instagram is a masterclass in "Usability" through intuitive gestures. You don't need a manual to use it—you tap to like, swipe to see stories, and pinch to zoom.
AestheticAesthetics refers to the visual appeal of the design. This is about how pleasing the design looks but also how it communicates meaning through visual elements like colour, typography, layout, and imagery.Duolingo uses aesthetics to turn a "chore" (learning a language) into a "game." Its bright colors, rounded buttons, and the famous "Duo" owl create a friendly, low-pressure environment that keeps users coming back.

Part 3: Deep Dive — Typography

What is typography?

Typography is the art of arranging letters and text in a way that makes the copy legible, clear, and visually appealing to the reader.

Legibility

The legibility of a typeface is a product of its design, and relates to the ability to distinguish one glyph from another when reading.

Readability

Readability is related to how the type is arranged, or typeset. The choice of typeface, the design and layout can create a more (or less) readable experience.

The Basics — Typeface vs Font

A common misconception: Typeface and Font are not the same thing. Understanding the distinction helps you communicate precisely about type.

TypefaceFont
Typeface: The family — e.g., 'Helvetica'Font: A specific style within a typeface
Think of it like a family name or brandE.g., 'Helvetica Bold 14pt' is a font
Helvetica is a typeface. So is Arial, Georgia, Inter.Weight + Style + Size = Font

Type Classifications

Anatomy of the typeface.

As UX designers, we control shape through four variables:

Font family — the overall design of the letterforms (serif, sans-serif, etc.)

Font weight — how thick or thin the strokes appear.

Font style — variations such as italic.

Letter case — whether text appears in uppercase, lowercase, or small caps.

Serif Typefaces: The Trustworthy Traditionalist

Serif typefaces, characterized by their small decorative strokes, have dominated print media for centuries.

Excels with long-form text because the serifs create a horizontal flow that guides the eye naturally from letter to letter. This makes serif fonts ideal for books, articles, and extended reading experiences.

Sans-Serif Typefaces: The Modern Minimalist

Modern look and feel that aligns with contemporary design aesthetics. Clean lines and geometric shapes create a sense of forward-thinking innovation.

Clean and simple appearance that works exceptionally well on screens, where pixel density and rendering can make serif details appear muddy or unclear.

Choosing the Right Font

Think about Personality

What project will the typeface be used for. And is the project formal or informal? Contemporary or traditional? Fun or serious? The typeface needs to match the mood.

It’s imperative that the typography reflects the personality of the brand or product.

Reflect on tone

It’s equally important to consider how the font harmonizes with the tone of the message.

It’s imperative that the typography reflects the personality of the brand or product.

Don’t skimp on function

there’s nothing worse than a website that looks pretty but is entirely illegible, leading you to click the wrong button or take a wrong turn because the instructions were unclear.

Part 4: Deep Dive — Visual Hierarchy

What is Visual Hierarchy?

Visual hierarchy is the arrangement of elements in a way that implies importance. It's the designer's way of saying: start here, then look here, then here. Without it, users don't know where to begin — and they'll disengage.

Part 5: Deep Dive — Negative Space

What is White Space?

“White space is not merely blank space — it’s an essential element that provides structure and balance to design.”

White space, also known as negative space, is the space between design elements. It includes margins, padding, line spacing, and the space between individual UI components. Despite its name, white space doesn’t necessarily have to be white — it can be any background color, texture, or even blurred imagery.

Macro White Space

The large spaces between major UI sections, such as sections of a webpage.

Micro White Space

The smaller spaces between elements like text lines, buttons, and icons.

Part 6: What is Color?

In the realm of User Interface (UI) and User Experience (UX) design, color is more than just an aesthetic choice; it’s a powerful tool that influences user perceptions, emotions, and behaviors.

The psychology of color plays a significant role in shaping how users interact with digital platforms, impacting their decisions, engagement, and overall experience. By understanding the psychological effects of different colors, designers can strategically employ color palettes to create visually appealing and emotionally resonant interfaces.

In this exploration, we delve into the psychology of color in UI/UX design and uncover the ways in which color choices can profoundly impact user interactions.
