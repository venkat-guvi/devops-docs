Part 1: Introduction to Product Strategy

What is Product Strategy ?

Product strategy is the plan that connects a product to a business goal. It answers three questions: Who are we building this for? What problem are we solving? Why will our solution win in the market?

Without a product strategy, a team builds features. With a product strategy, a team builds a product that matters.

Why it matters

Gives direction to design and development

Aligns teams toward a common goal

Helps prioritize features

Ensures product-market fit

Key Components of a Product Strategy

Vision & Objectives: The long-term impact of the product and how it aligns with company goals.

Target Audience (Personas): Defines who the product serves and their specific needs.

Value Proposition: Explains how the product solves user problems better than alternatives.

Product Roadmap: The actionable, high-level plan detailing the journey from concept to market.

Key Metrics (KPIs): Measurable goals for evaluating success.

Why UX Designers Must Understand Product Strategy

The Levels of Strategy

Product strategy does not exist in isolation. It sits within a hierarchy of strategic decisions. Understanding this hierarchy helps you know where your UX work fits and who makes which decisions.

Level

What It Defines

Example

Company Strategy

The overall direction and purpose of the organisation

We will become the leading platform for freelance work in Southeast Asia

Business Unit Strategy

How a business unit or product line competes in its market

Our mobile product will win the under-30 segment through speed and simplicity

Product Strategy

Which problems to solve, for whom, and how

We will focus on onboarding friction for first-time users in Q3 and Q4

UX Strategy

How design supports the product strategy

Reduce onboarding drop-off by 30% through progressive disclosure and contextual help

Feature Strategy

Which capabilities to build and when

Launch a guided setup wizard before the free trial ends

Part 2: User Needs vs Business Goals vs Technical Feasibility

The most powerful framework in product strategy is also the simplest: the overlap of three forces. Every successful product lives at the intersection of what users need, what the business requires, and what the technology can actually deliver.

User Needs:

Functional needs: What task is the user trying to complete? What outcome do they need?

Emotional needs: How do they want to feel during and after the experience?

Social needs: How does the product affect their relationships, status, or identity?

User needs

Explanation

Jobs to be done

What progress is the user trying to make in their life or work?

Hierarchy of needs

Not all needs are equal - some are baseline expectations, others are differentiators

Research requirement

User needs must be evidenced, not assumed. Personas, interview insights, and journey maps are the evidence base for the desirability lens.

Business Goals:

Acquisition: Conversion rate, onboarding completion, trial-to-paid conversion - all UX-driven metrics

Retention: Daily/monthly active users, churn rate, net promoter score - UX is the primary lever

Engagement: Feature adoption, session length, task completion rate - directly tied to design quality

Revenue: Average order value, upsell rate, premium feature adoption - UX drives these through clarity and trust

Cost reduction: Support ticket volume, user error rates, self-serve resolution - better UX reduces support cost

Brand: First impressions, share-of-voice, design awards - UX builds brand equity at every touchpoint

Technical Feasibility

Platform capabilities: What can iOS, Android, or the web actually do? Native components exist for a reason.

Performance: Animations, large images, and real-time data all have performance costs - beautiful can be slow

Data availability: You cannot design a personalisation feature if the data to power it does not exist

Third-party dependencies: Payments, maps, authentication - these constrain the UX you can offer

Engineering capacity: A technically perfect design that takes 6 months to build may not be the right design for this sprint

Best practice: Include engineering early in design reviews. A 5-minute conversation can save days of rework.

Conflict Scenario

What It Looks Like

Strategic Response

Users want free access, but the business needs money.

Users don’t want to pay for the app, but the company needs revenue.

Offer a free version with limits or ads, and a paid version for full access.

Users want a feature, but it’s hard to build.

Users expect fast or real-time features, but the system is not ready.

Provide a simpler version now and improve it later.

Business wants speed, but development takes time.

The company wants to launch quickly, but full features need more time.

Launch a basic version (MVP) with only important features.

All three in conflict: User needs, business goals, and tech limits all clash.

Users want more features, business wants profit, tech can’t support everything.

Focus on the core feature and build the simplest solution that works for all.

Part 3: Value Proposition Design

A value proposition is the specific benefit a product delivers to a specific user in a way that no competitor can match as well. It is the single most important strategic statement a product team can make - and the one most teams get wrong.

A value proposition clearly explains:

Who your product is for

What problem it solves

Why it is better than other options

Simple Formula

We help [users] to [solve problems] by [solutions].

Example:We help busy people order food quickly by giving a fast reorder option.

The Customer Segment

The Customer Profile is the starting point and it is where you are capturing the insights and needs from your customer research. I recommend that different profiles (and Value Propositions) are created for distinct customer types to get really clear on the products and services that provide value.

Customer Jobs

Customer jobs are the things users are trying to do or achieve when they use a product.

Job Type

Definition

Example (Food Delivery App)

Functional Job

What the user wants to do

Order food without having to cook or go out

Emotional Job

How the user wants to feel during or after the experience

Feel relaxed and stress-free after ordering

Social Job

How the user wants to be perceived by others

Appear like a good host when ordering for a group

Supporting Job

Secondary tasks that enable the primary job

Track the delivery status without anxiety

Gains and Pains

Gains are the outcomes users want - the benefits they expect, desire, or would be surprised and delighted by. Pains are the risks, obstacles, and negative experiences they want to avoid.

GAINS (Outcomes Users Want)

PAINS (Outcomes Users Want to Avoid)

Expected gains: Basic needs

Obstacles: Things that block users Example: Confusing app

Desired gains: Better experience

Risks: Things users worry aboutExample: Bad quality food

Unexpected gains: Surprise features

Frustrations: Bad past experiences

Example: Wrong order delivered

Example: Food app - expected: food arrives hot. Desired: food arrives in under 30 mins. Unexpected: the app knows their dietary preferences before they order.

Example: Food app - pain: not knowing if the restaurant is actually good. Pain: order arriving wrong. Pain: being charged for delivery without clear notice.

Part 4: Identifying Opportunity Areas

An opportunity area is a space in the market or user experience where a meaningful unmet need exists and where a well-designed solution could create significant value. Identifying opportunities is how strategy moves from understanding to direction.

Four Types of Opportunity Areas

Users might say they love a feature, but do they actually use it? Behavioral research focuses on answering that question.

Underserved Needs

Users have clear needs that no current solution adequately addresses. High user pain, low current satisfaction.

Market Gaps

Segments or use cases competitors have ignored. Often visible through secondary research and complaint analysis.

Emerging Behaviors

New patterns driven by technology shifts, cultural change, or regulation. Early movers gain outsized advantage.

Friction Points

Places in the current user journey where effort is disproportionately high relative to value delivered.

Four Types of Opportunity Areas

Opportunity areas do not announce themselves. They must be surfaced through structured investigation. The following methods are the most reliable and practical for UX-led teams.

Method

What It Surfaces

How to Use It

User Interview Analysis

Unmet needs, workarounds, frustrations, and aspirations directly from users

Conduct 5-8 interviews, code for recurring themes, identify where current solutions fall short

Journey Mapping

Friction points, emotional low points, and gaps across the end-to-end experience

Map the current state journey, mark all pain points, look for clusters of friction as opportunity signals

App Store Review Mining

What real users complain about and praise - unfiltered, at scale

Sort competitor reviews by recency and rating, find recurring complaints across 3+ competitors - these are market-level opportunities

Analytics Review

Drop-off points, underused features, and abandoned flows

Identify where users disengage or fail to complete key tasks - high exit rates signal unmet needs

Analogous Domain Research

Solutions from adjacent industries that could be adapted

Study how other industries solve similar problems - healthcare scheduling, logistics tracking, financial onboarding

Part 5: Competitive Analysis

Competitive analysis is the process of systematically studying competing and adjacent products to understand the market landscape, identify your own strengths and gaps, and make more informed product and design decisions.

Why Competitive Analysis Matters for UX

Establishes market context: Users compare your product to alternatives - knowingly and unknowingly

Identifies UI conventions users have already learned - breaking them has a usability cost

Reveals what competitors have solved well and what they have failed to solve

Prevents reinventing the wheel for standard patterns (e.g. checkout flows, onboarding)

Identifies white space: what no one has done well that you could own

Informs differentiation: knowing what competitors offer helps you design for what they do not

Types of Competitors

Competitor Type

Definition

Example (For a Budgeting App)

Direct Competitors

Products that solve the exact same problem for the same users

Other budgeting apps: YNAB, Mint, Money Manager

Indirect Competitors

Products that solve the same user job through a different approach

Spreadsheets, pen-and-paper notebooks, bank apps with spending insights

Adjacent Competitors

Products in related spaces that could expand into your market

Investment apps like Zerodha or Groww that could add budgeting features

Analogous Products

Products from different industries with a similar UX pattern to learn from

Fitness tracking apps for habit-building patterns; subscription apps for payment flow patterns

6 UX Competitive Analysis Research Methods

SWOT Analysis

SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats) is an analysis technique companies can use internally or against the competition. Companies can conduct a SWOT analysis on an entire industry, market, competitor, product range, or a single product.

Using a Competitor’s Product

One of the easiest ways to “spy” on your competition and gather data is using their products. For example:

Firstly, what are your competitor’s touchpoints? What happens when you land on their website, download the app, read a blog article, etc.? How does the competition turn traffic into users and then paying customers?

How does your competitor present its products and pricing to customers?

What happens when you sign up for a free trial?

How easy is it to upgrade? And more importantly, do they make it easy to cancel–what’s that process like?

Analyze the overall UI design, including layout, microinteractions, colors, typography, etc.

Use the product as a customer to complete tasks. Were there any pain points? What does your competitor do well and poorly?

Reading Competitors’ Reviews

Reviews from mobile app stores, social media (Facebook pages, Twitter mentions), marketplaces, and websites like TrustPilot are excellent resources for analyzing competitors (and also your product’s UX). These customer reviews allow you to find out what customers love and hate about your competition.

Comparison Chart

Comparison charts are best for direct competitors that offer similar product features. For example, you might want to compare a paid plan to your competitors to determine which company offers customers the most value.

User Journey Comparison

User journeys map how customers complete tasks from start to end. Optimizing this end-to-end process can enhance the user experience and increase conversions.

Usability Test on a Competitor’s Prototype

One way to compare the competition is by building a prototype replica of their product or flow to see how users interact and engage with it. Designers can use these insights to revise their designs and make improvements.

Part 6: Strategy Alignment in UX

Strategy alignment in UX means ensuring that every design decision - every screen, every flow, every interaction - can be traced back to a strategic goal. It is what separates design that is merely beautiful from design that moves the business forward.

Presenting UX Work in Strategic Terms

Designer Language (Less Effective)

Strategic Language (More Effective)

"We improved the visual design of the checkout page."

"We reduced checkout abandonment by simplifying the form from 14 fields to 6, which we project will improve conversion by 15-20%."

"We conducted 8 user interviews and found 12 insights."

"Research with 8 users identified 3 critical friction points in onboarding that are likely causing the 60% Day-1 drop-off."

"The new onboarding has a better user experience."

"The new onboarding reduces time-to-first-value from 8 minutes to 2, which should improve Day-7 retention."

"Users prefer the redesigned navigation."

"Users found core features 40% faster with the new navigation, which should increase feature adoption."

💡 Key Takeaways

Product Strategy

A plan that connects a product to a business goal by answering: Who is it for, what problem does it solve, and why will it win?

Three-Lens Framework

Every successful product lives at the intersection of User Needs (Desirability), Business Goals (Viability), and Technical Feasibility.

Value Proposition

A specific, testable claim about the outcome a product delivers to a specific user in a way that no competitor matches as well.

Value Proposition Canvas

A tool with two sides - the Customer Profile (jobs, gains, pains) and the Value Map (products, gain creators, pain relievers) - fit is when they align.

Opportunity Areas

Spaces where user needs are unmet by current solutions - found through research, journey mapping, analytics, and competitive analysis.

Competitive Analysis

A structured evaluation of competing products to identify conventions to respect, gaps to fill, and white space to own.

Strategy Alignment

Every UX decision should connect to a strategic goal and be frameable in terms of the business outcome it serves.
