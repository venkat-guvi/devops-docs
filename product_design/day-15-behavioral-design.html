Part 1: Introduction to Behavioral Design

What is Behavioral Design?

Behavioral design is the practice of applying knowledge from psychology, cognitive science, and behavioral economics to intentionally shape how people think, feel, and act when using a product. It is the intersection of how the human mind actually works and how we design for it.

It is not about manipulating users. At its core, it is about reducing friction, supporting user goals, and creating experiences that feel intuitive — because they are aligned with how humans naturally behave.

Where Behavioral Design Appears in Products

Onboarding flows that guide new users to their first 'aha moment' without cognitive overload

Notification systems timed to moments when users are most receptive

Checkout flows designed to reduce abandonment through trust signals and progress cues

Habit-forming apps (fitness, language learning, meditation) that keep users returning daily

E-commerce product pages using social proof, scarcity, and anchoring to influence purchase decisions

Part 2: Cognitive Psychology in UX

Core Cognitive Laws Every UX Designer Must Know

Hick's Law

The time it takes a user to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices available.

More options = longer decision time = higher abandonment rate

Design implication: Reduce the number of choices at each decision point. Use progressive disclosure — reveal options as needed, not all at once.

Example: Netflix limits the visible title count on the home screen. Showing 200 titles simultaneously causes paralysis.

Fitts's Law

The time required to reach a target is a function of the distance to the target and the size of the target. Larger targets closer to the user's starting position are faster to reach.

Design implication: Make primary CTAs large and placed where thumbs naturally rest on mobile (bottom of screen)

Design implication: Destructive actions (delete, cancel) should be small and far — not the dominant tap target

Example: iOS and Android place primary navigation at the bottom of the screen precisely because it is thumb-reachable

Miller's Law

The average human can hold approximately 7 (plus or minus 2) items in working memory at one time.

Design implication: Group information into chunks of 5–7 items maximum per screen section

Design implication: Multi-step forms should be broken into logical groupings, not presented as a single long page

Example: Phone numbers are formatted as 98765-43210, not 9876543210 — chunking makes them memorable

Jakob's Law

Users spend most of their time on other apps. They expect your app to work the same way as the apps they already know.

Design implication: Follow established UX conventions — don't reinvent navigation, icons, or gestures without a compelling reason

Design implication: Innovation in interaction costs the user cognitive effort. Reserve it for where it creates genuine value.

Example: The hamburger menu (☰) is universally understood. Creating a novel icon for navigation forces users to learn a new pattern.

The Law of Proximity

Items that are visually close together are perceived as related. Items that are further apart are perceived as unrelated.

Design implication: Group related form fields, actions, and content visually. Don't let related items be separated by whitespace or unrelated elements.

Example: A 'Submit' button placed far from the form it submits creates a spatial disconnect that causes hesitation.

Cognitive Load

Cognitive load is the total mental effort required to use an interface. There are three types:

Intrinsic load: Complexity inherent to the task itself (filling in tax details is complex — this can't be removed)

Extraneous load: Complexity added by poor design (confusing layout, unclear labels, inconsistent patterns — always remove this)

Germane load: Mental effort that builds skill or understanding — good tutorials add this intentionally

Part 3: Fogg Behavior Model

The Fogg Behavior Model, developed by BJ Fogg at Stanford University's Persuasive Technology Lab, provides a simple, powerful formula for understanding when and why human behavior occurs.

The formula: B = MAP

Behavior happens when Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt converge at the same moment.

If a target behavior is NOT happening, at least one of the three components is missing or insufficient. The model gives designers a diagnostic framework — not just a design framework.

ComponentDescriptionDesign Examples
MotivationThe energy and desire to perform a behaviour. It drives whether a person WANTS to act.Curiosity, fear, social acceptance, hope, pleasure, reward
AbilityHow easy it is to perform the behaviour. Simpler = more likely. Complexity kills action.Reduce steps, autofill, defaults, progressive disclosure, time saved
PromptThe signal that triggers action at the right moment. Without a prompt, even motivated, able users may not act.Push notifications, tooltips, badges, empty states, progress bars

Using the Fogg Model to Diagnose Design Problems

When a desired behavior is not happening, ask these three diagnostic questions:

Is Motivation sufficient? Does the user understand why they should take this action? Is the benefit clear and compelling?

Is Ability sufficient? Is the task too complex, too long, or too unfamiliar? Can we simplify, automate, or scaffold?

Is there a Prompt? Does the user know when and how to start? Is there a visible, timely trigger?

Problem: Users not completing sign-upFogg Model Solution
Motivation gap: Value not clearShow what the user gets immediately after sign-up
Ability gap: Too many required fieldsReduce to 3 fields minimum; use social login
Prompt gap: No entry CTA on landingAdd a persistent 'Get Started' CTA above the fold
Motivation gap: Fear of spam/dataAdd 'No credit card · Cancel anytime' reassurance
Ability gap: Password requirements unclearShow requirements inline, in real-time, as the user types

Part 4: Hook Model — Habit Formation

The Hook Model

The Hook Model, developed by Nir Eyal in his book 'Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products' (2014), describes the four-phase cycle through which products create habitual user behavior. It is the most widely referenced model of user engagement in the technology industry.

PhaseWhat It IsReal-World ExampleExamples
1. TRIGGERWhat makes users open the productExternal triggers start behavior, but internal triggers build habitsExternal: Notifications, ads, emailsInternal: Bored → Instagram, Anxious → Twitter, Morning → Duolingo
2. ACTIONThe simplest thing user doesIf it’s not easy, users won’t do it✔ Infinite scroll (just swipe)✔ Search (type 1 word)❌ Login wall before value
3. VARIABLE REWARDWhat users get after actionUnpredictable rewards keep users hookedTribe: Likes, commentsHunt: Search results, new contentSelf: Streaks, XP, progress
4. INVESTMENTWhat users put into the productMore investment = harder to leaveLinkedIn connectionsSpotify recommendationsDuolingo streaks

Part 5: Persuasive Design + Emotional Design

Persuasive Design Techniques

Persuasive design uses established principles of human psychology to guide users toward actions that serve both the user's and the product's goals. Robert Cialdini's 6 Principles of Influence (from 'Influence', 1984) are the most widely applied framework.

PrincipleHow It Appears in UXExamples
Reciprocity — Give first, ask later'Try for free 30 days' — value delivered before commitmentSpotify 1 month free
Commitment & Consistency — Start smallProgress bars, onboarding steps, 'you've completed 20%'LinkedIn profile completeness bar
Social Proof — Others are doing it'4.8 stars · 12,000 reviews', 'Trending in your area'Zomato restaurant rating + review count
Authority — Credibility signalsTrust badges, expert endorsements, certification logosBooking.com trust badge
Liking — People say yes to those they likeFriendly tone, mascots (Duolingo owl), personalised greetingsDuolingo character greeting.
Scarcity — Fear of missing out'Only 2 rooms left', 'Offer ends in 23:14'MakeMyTrip 'Only 1 seat left'

Emotional Design

Don Norman's three levels of emotional design (from 'Emotional Design', 2003) describe how users experience and bond with products at different psychological depths.

LevelsExplanation
Visceral DesignImmediate, unconscious aesthetic and sensory response. Before any thought occurs, the brain makes a positive or negative judgment.
Behavioral DesignThe pleasure and effectiveness experienced while using the product. This is the domain most UX designers focus on.
Reflective DesignThe conscious, narrative meaning users construct about the product and themselves. The story a user tells about why they use this product.

Part 6 : Dark Patterns vs Ethical UX

Dark patterns are user interface design choices that trick, mislead, or coerce users into doing things they did not intend to do — often benefiting the business at the direct cost of the user. The term was coined by UX designer Harry Brignull in 2010.

Dark patterns are not accidents. They are deliberate design decisions, often made under business pressure, that exploit the same cognitive biases and behavioral principles covered in this session — but for the company's gain, not the user's.

Dark PatternWhat It DoesReal-World Example
Roach MotelEasy to sign up, impossible to cancel. User trapped inside a product.Many subscription services bury the cancel option 6 menus deep.
Confirm-shamingGuilt-trip language on the 'decline' option to pressure acceptance.Opt-out button says 'No thanks, I don't want to save money'.
Hidden CostsExtra fees revealed only at the final checkout step after time invested.Booking sites adding resort fees, taxes, and 'convenience' fees at checkout.
Trick QuestionsConfusingly worded opt-in/opt-out checkboxes designed to mislead.Double-negative phrasing: 'Uncheck to not receive marketing emails'.
Bait and SwitchUser selects one action but a different, unwanted action occurs.Download button triggers an installer for unrelated software.
Forced ContinuityFree trial silently converts to paid subscription without clear notice.Credit card required upfront; charge happens with no reminder email.
Social Proof SpamFake or manipulated social signals to create false consensus.'1,847 people viewed this in the last hour' — unverifiable urgency.
Disguised AdsAds designed to look like editorial content or search results.Paid results styled identically to organic content without clear labelling.

Ethical Persuasion Vs Dark Pattern

Ethical PersuasionDark Pattern
Urgency based on real availabilityFabricated countdown timers and fake scarcity
Social proof from real, verified reviewsFake review counts and unverifiable 'X people viewing'
Easy opt-out, clearly labelledHidden unsubscribe links and confirm-shaming opt-outs
One-click cancellation processMulti-step cancellation buried in account settings
Clear pricing with all fees upfrontHidden fees revealed only at final checkout step
Honest push notifications with opt-outGuilt-driven re-engagement: 'You've abandoned your streak'

Ethical UX — The Designer's Responsibility

Ethical UX is not the absence of behavioral design — it is the application of behavioral design in ways that genuinely serve users, respect their autonomy, and are transparent about their intent. The same psychological principles can be used ethically or unethically depending on whose interests they serve.

Ethical PrincipleWhat It Means in Practice
TransparencyBe honest about what your product does, costs, and collects. No hidden agendas.
User ControlGive users meaningful choices — including the choice to opt out, delete, and leave.
Informed ConsentExplain what users are agreeing to in plain language, not legal boilerplate.
ReversibilityMake it easy to undo, cancel, and change decisions. Don't trap users.
Honest UrgencyOnly create urgency when it is real. Never fabricate scarcity or time pressure.
Respect for AttentionNotifications and interruptions should earn their place — used sparingly.
Inclusive DesignBehavioural patterns should work for all users, not exploit vulnerable groups.
