Part 1: Low vs High Fidelity Wireframes

1.1 What is a Wireframe?

A wireframe is a simplified representation of a screen or page. It shows the structure and layout of content without detailed design (colours, images, typography). Wireframes focus on where things go and how they are organized — not what they look like.

"Wireframes are blueprints for how a screen is structured. They answer: where does content go? What is the hierarchy? Not: what colour is the button?"

1.2 The Fidelity Spectrum

Wireframes exist on a spectrum from lo-fi (rough sketches) to hi-fi (pixel-perfect mockups). The fidelity level affects how quickly they are created, who understands them, and what feedback they generate.

FidelityTime to CreateToolsBest ForDetails Shown
Low-Fi (Sketches)MinutesPaper, pen, whiteboard, Balsamiq, Wireframe.ccEarly exploration, communicating ideas quickly, getting team alignmentBoxes for content, basic labels, no colours/images
Low-Fi (Digital)Minutes to hoursFigma, Adobe XD, Sketch (basic frames)Layout exploration, sharing rough ideas, documentationSimple rectangles, minimal text, grayscale
Mid-Fi (Wireframes)HoursFigma, Adobe XD, Sketch with annotationsStructural validation, developer handoff, detailed workflowsClear content areas, labels, interactions noted, maybe basic states
High-Fi (Mockups)Hours to daysFigma, Adobe XD, Sketch with design systemDesign feedback, stakeholder buy-in, high-fidelity prototypingReal colours, typography, images, icons, detailed spacing
Interactive (Prototype)Days to weeksFigma interactive mode, Principle, Framer, codeTesting interactions, demonstrating flows, gathering user feedbackAll hi-fi details + clickable, animated, working states

1.3 Low-Fidelity Wireframes: When & Why

When to Use Low-Fidelity Wireframes
Early exploration: You are exploring multiple layout options. Quick iterations matter more than perfection.
Getting alignment: You need stakeholder or team buy-in on structure before investing time in design.
Rapid prototyping: You want to test a flow quickly with users (with rough screens, not polished ones).
Communication: You need to explain an idea to someone (easier to understand a sketch than a complex mockup).
When details do not matter yet: The exact colour/font/spacing can wait. Structure is the priority.
Preventing design lock: Low-fi work feels less "final", so people are more comfortable suggesting changes.

1.4 High-Fidelity Wireframes (Mockups): When & Why

When to Use High-Fidelity Mockups
Design system validation: You want to ensure every element uses system components and tokens correctly.
Handoff to engineering: Developers need pixel-perfect specs, spacing, colours, component properties.
Design feedback: You want detailed critique on visual design, not just structure.
Stakeholder approval: Executives/stakeholders want to see how it really looks before development.
A/B testing prep: You are preparing designs to be coded into prototypes for user testing.
Accessibility review: You want to validate colour contrast, text readability, interactive element sizes.
Brand consistency: You need to verify the design matches brand guidelines throughout.

Part 2: Interaction & Navigation Design

Duration: 20 minutes

2.1 What is Interaction Design?

Interaction design is how users move through your product. It includes what happens when they click a button, submit a form, scroll, swipe, or perform any action. Wireframes should document these interactions, not just show static layouts.

SLIDE: Interaction Design Basics
Interactive elements (buttons, links, inputs) must be clearly identifiable
User actions (click, tap, scroll, drag) must have visible feedback
Navigation must be clear (where can I go from here?)
Affordances must be obvious (this looks clickable because...)
Consistency matters (the same action behaves the same way everywhere)
States matter (how does this look when active/disabled/loading/error?)
Transitions guide the eye (not jarring, not confusing)
Feedback confirms user actions (did my click register?)

2.2 Key Interaction Patterns

PatternHow It WorksWhen to UseDesign Notes
Tap/ClickUser taps element, action occurs (submit, navigate, toggle)Any button, link, form elementMust be obvious it is clickable (colour, underline, shadow)
ToggleUser taps to turn something on/off (Settings, filters, favorite)Binary states (yes/no, on/off)Clearly show current state (highlight, change colour)
ScrollUser scrolls to see more content (infinite scroll, long pages)Content that does not fit on screenShow scrollbar or hint there is more content below
SwipeUser swipes left/right to change content or navigate (carousels, galleries, stories)Showing alternatives sequentiallyMobile-first. Show indicators (dots, numbers) showing position
Drag & DropUser drags item to new location (reorder, move to trash, kanban board)Reorganizing, complex interactionsShow visual feedback (highlight, hover state, ghost element)
HoverUser hovers mouse over element (tooltip appears, button changes colour)Desktop interactions only (no mobile)Reveal additional info or change visual style
Multi-Step FormUser fills form across multiple screens (checkout, signup wizard)Long forms where all fields do not fit on one screenShow progress (step 2 of 5), allow going back, save progress
Modal / DialogUser interacts with overlay that blocks background (delete confirmation, alert)Requiring user attention or confirmationProvide clear action buttons (Confirm/Cancel), easy to close

2.3 Affordances: Making Actions Obvious

An affordance is a design feature that suggests how an object should be used. A button that looks like a button (raised, coloured, with text) affords clicking. A link that is underlined affords clicking. Without affordances, users do not know what is interactive.

Looks Clickable (Good Affordance)Looks Static (Poor Affordance)
Coloured background (stands out from text)Text-only link, no underline (might miss it)
Raised shadow or border (looks pressable)Grey-on-grey low contrast (is this clickable?)
Cursor changes to pointer on hoverNo hover state (nothing changes when mouse over)
Text is white on dark, high contrastSame style as all text (no visual distinction)
Rounded corners signal interactive elementHidden inside paragraph (user does not notice)
Prominent placement (above the fold)No visual feedback (did my click work?)

Part 3: Prototyping Concepts

Duration: 25 minutes

3.1 Wireframe vs Mockup vs Prototype

These terms are often confused. Here is the distinction:

ArtifactDefinitionHas Interactions?When CreatedUsed For
WireframeLow to mid-fidelity layout showing content hierarchy and structureNotes only (e.g. "click here to open menu")Early design phaseCommunication, alignment, structure validation
MockupHigh-fidelity visual design with colours, typography, images, spacingNo (static screenshot)Mid design phaseDesign feedback, handoff, brand alignment
PrototypeInteractive, clickable version that demonstrates flows and interactionsYes (actually clickable/functional)Late design phase, before developmentUser testing, demonstrating flows, gathering feedback
CodeActual built application or websiteYes (fully functional)Development phaseProduction, final testing, deployment

3.2 When to Prototype

SLIDE: Prototype When You Have...
Uncertain interactions: "Will users understand this flow?" Build a prototype to test.
Complex workflows: Multi-step processes. Prototypes show the full journey.
Animation or transitions: Static mockups cannot show "how does it move?"
Real data: You want to test with actual content or numbers.
Stakeholder skepticism: A working prototype convinces better than a mockup.
User research plan: You want to test ideas with real users before development.
Team alignment questions: Everyone has a different idea of what "swipe" means. Show them.
Before major implementation: Prototyping saves development time by validating ideas early.

3.3 Prototyping Fidelity vs Testing Goal

Different fidelity levels serve different testing purposes. Match the fidelity to your goal:

Test Navigation & FlowTest Visual Design & Brand
Use: Low to mid-fidelity prototypeUse: High-fidelity prototype (near pixel-perfect)
Focus: Can users complete the task? Do they understand the flow?Focus: Do users like the design? Does it feel premium? Does it match brand?
Example: Clickable wireframe of checkout (3 screens, no colours)Example: Fully designed prototype with brand colours, images, animations
Feedback: "I was confused at step 2" or "I was not sure which button to click"Feedback: "This feels too corporate" or "I love the transition animations"
Time investment: Few hours (quick iteration matters more than polish)Time investment: More hours (polish matters for accurate feedback)

Part 4: Screen Transitions & UX Patterns

Duration: 20 minutes

4.1 What Are Screen Transitions?

A transition is how one screen exits and the next screen enters. It guides the user's eye and creates a sense of continuity. Transitions should feel natural, not jarring. They should communicate spatial relationships (was I going deeper into the app, or going back?).

4.2 Common Transition Types

TransitionHow It FeelsBest ForSpeed
FadeOne screen fades out, new screen fades in. Neutral, no spatial relationship implied.Switching between unrelated screens or contexts200-300ms
Slide (Push)New screen slides in from right, pushing old screen out. Implies going deeper/forward.Navigating deeper into hierarchy (tapping a product)300-400ms
Slide (Pop)Current screen slides out to right, reveals previous screen behind. Implies going back.Back button, going up hierarchy300-400ms
ScaleNew screen grows from a tapped element, shrinks when closing. Emphasizes source.Opening a modal or detail view from a list250-350ms
FlipScreen flips to reveal next screen. Implies same context, two sides of same thing.Card flipping (front/back), toggling between views300-400ms
ParallaxBackground and foreground move at different speeds. Creates depth.Scrolling pages with layered elementsContinuous (speed of scroll)
No transitionInstant switch. No animation. Fast.When speed matters more than smoothness (mobile, slow devices)0ms

4.3 Micro-Interactions: The Small Moments

Micro-interactions are tiny animations or feedback moments that delight users and confirm their actions. They are not essential, but they make the experience feel polished and responsive.

Common Micro-Interactions
Button press feedback: Button scales down 2% when clicked, gives tactile feeling
Loading spinner: Animated spinner during data load, tells user "something is happening"
Empty state illustration: When list is empty, show friendly illustration + hint to create first item
Pull-to-refresh: Drag page down and it "springs" back with refreshed data. Satisfying.
Shake for error: Form shakes gently when validation fails. Attention-grabbing but not aggressive.
Checkmark animation: Form input gets green checkmark with animation when valid. Satisfying.
Notification toast: Quick notification slides in from top/bottom, disappears after 3 seconds
Floating action button: FAB has hover state, pulse animation to draw attention

4.4 Common UX Patterns

SLIDE: Patterns Every Designer Should Know
Tab Navigation: Shows related content in tabs (Desktop: top tabs. Mobile: bottom tabs).
Hamburger Menu: Three horizontal lines that reveal navigation (mobile standard).
Floating Action Button (FAB): Circular button for primary action (Create, Compose, Add).
Infinite Scroll: Scroll to bottom, more content loads automatically (social feeds, image galleries).
Pagination: "Showing 1-20 of 500. Next/Previous buttons" (better for some contexts than infinite scroll).
Accordion: Sections expand/collapse to save space. Exactly one section open at a time.
Modal Dialog: Overlay that requires user attention before proceeding (delete confirmation, alerts).
Empty State: Show helpful message when no data exists yet (empty inbox, empty search results).
Loading State: Show progress (spinner, skeleton, progress bar) during data load.
Undo/Redo: Allow users to undo actions (restore deleted item, undo text edit).

Session Recap & Connection to Reality

How This All Connects

SLIDE: From Wireframe to Shipped Product
Low-Fi Wireframe: Explore layout with stakeholders. Quick feedback. "Does this structure make sense?"
High-Fi Mockup: Polish the design. Apply system. Brand check. "Does this match our brand?"
Interactive Prototype: Add interactions, transitions, states. "Does the flow work? Do animations feel right?"
Usability Testing: Users test the prototype. Gather feedback. "Where are users confused? Where do they succeed?"
Refine: Fix problems. Iterate. Test again. "Better now?"
Handoff: Give mockups + prototype + design system to engineering. "Here is exactly what to build."
Development: Engineers build the real product. "Your prototype matches the product almost exactly."
QA & Testing: Final user testing in production. Measure metrics. "Did it work?"
Launch: Release to users. Monitor real usage.
Iterate: Real user data drives next improvements.
Key Principles
Wireframes communicate structure; mockups communicate design; prototypes test interactions.
Affordances must be obvious: if users do not know something is clickable, they will not click it.
Transitions should guide the eye and communicate spatial relationships, not distract.
Micro-interactions delight users and confirm their actions. They are the polish that makes a product feel premium.
Use patterns that users already know. Do not invent new interaction paradigms unless there is a good reason.
Prototype early, test often. A rough prototype tested with users beats a perfect mockup that sits in Figma.
Match fidelity to testing goal. Test navigation with lo-fi prototypes. Test visual design with hi-fi.
Transitions and animations should be 200-400ms. Too fast and users do not see it. Too slow and it feels sluggish.
