Module 1 — Introduction to Usability Testing

What Is Usability Testing?

Usability testing is a research method where real users attempt real tasks on a product — a website, app, or prototype — while a designer or researcher observes how easily they succeed. The goal is not to test the user; it is to test the design. Every point of confusion, hesitation, or failure reveals an opportunity to improve the product before it reaches a wider audience.

Unlike opinion-based feedback, usability testing is grounded in observed behavior. It answers the question “Can people actually use this?” rather than “Do people like this?”

Why Usability Testing Matters

Finds usability problems early, before they become expensive to fix

Validates design decisions with real evidence instead of assumptions

Improves user satisfaction and reduces frustration

Reduces development cost by catching issues before engineering builds them

Increases the overall chance of product success in the market

Usability Testing vs. Related Practices

Beginners often confuse usability testing with other UX activities. The table below clarifies the differences.

ActivityPrimary QuestionTypical Method
UI ReviewDoes the interface follow visual and design standards?Expert / heuristic walkthrough
UX EvaluationDoes the overall experience meet user needs?Heuristic evaluation, audits
User ResearchWho are our users and what do they need?Interviews, surveys, field studies
Usability TestingCan real users complete real tasks successfully?Task-based observation with users

Usability testing is not a one-time checkpoint at the end of a project — it is woven throughout the design process, from early research to post-launch refinement.

Why Testing Matters: Assumptions vs. Real Behavior

Even experienced designers cannot reliably predict how users will behave. Designers know the product too well to experience it the way a first-time user does — this is sometimes called the “curse of knowledge.” That's why designers should never rely solely on testing their own product themselves.

Assumptions vs. Real User Behavior — what a team expects users to do is often very different from what actually happens.

Why designers shouldn't test only by themselves — self-testing misses blind spots created by familiarity with the design.

Importance of user feedback — direct observation surfaces problems no amount of internal review can catch.

Figure 1.2 — The path designers expect users to take is rarely the path users actually take.

Module 2 — Types of Testing

There is no single “correct” way to run a usability test. The right method depends on your timeline, budget, and the questions you're trying to answer. This module walks through the most common approaches used in the industry today.

Moderated Testing

In moderated testing, a facilitator guides the participant through tasks in real time, asking follow-up questions and observing behavior as it happens.

A facilitator is present throughout the session

Observation happens live, allowing the facilitator to probe deeper

Real-time questions can clarify confusing moments as they occur

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Rich, in-depth insight; ability to ask follow-up questionsTime-consuming and requires a trained facilitator
Facilitator can redirect confused participantsFacilitator presence may slightly influence behavior

Figure 2.1 — A moderated session: the facilitator observes and asks the participant to think aloud while completing tasks.

Unmoderated Testing

Participants complete tasks independently, usually through a remote testing platform, without a facilitator present.

Users complete tasks entirely on their own

Typically conducted remotely, at the participant's convenience

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Faster and cheaper; can scale to many participantsNo opportunity to ask real-time follow-up questions
Participants behave naturally, without an observer presentHarder to clarify confusing or ambiguous results

Remote Testing

Remote testing allows researchers to test with participants anywhere in the world, without needing a physical lab.

Benefits

Access to a wider, more diverse pool of participants

Lower cost than in-person facilities and travel

Faster turnaround from recruitment to results

Common Tools

Maze • UXtweak • UserTesting • Lookback • Lyssna • Microsoft Teams • Google Meet

Figure 2.2 — A typical remote testing workflow, from recruitment to analysis.

Figure 2.3 — Common platforms fellows will encounter for remote and unmoderated usability testing.

In-Person Testing

In-person testing brings participants into a physical space — often a dedicated usability lab — where their session can be observed directly and recorded with specialized equipment.

When to Use

Best for complex products, physical devices, accessibility testing, or when stakeholders need to observe live sessions together.

AdvantagesLimitations
Highest-fidelity observation of body language and reactionsExpensive; requires a physical space and equipment
Easy for stakeholders to observe together in real timeLimited to participants who can travel to the location

Figure 2.4 — A typical in-person usability lab: a test room with the participant and device, observed from an adjoining room.

A/B Testing

A/B testing compares two versions of a design with real traffic to see which one performs better against a specific metric. Unlike moderated or unmoderated usability tests, A/B testing is quantitative and typically runs at scale, after a usability issue has already been identified and a solution proposed.

Purpose

To validate which design decision performs better in the real world — commonly used for:

Button color

Call-to-action (CTA) text

Page layout

Navigation style

Figure 2.5 — A simple A/B test comparing two checkout button treatments.

Guerrilla Testing

Guerrilla testing is a low-cost, informal method where designers approach people in public spaces — a coffee shop, a co-working space — and ask them to try a task for a few minutes in exchange for a small thank-you.

Extremely low-cost and fast to organize

Common at startups and in early-stage concept validation

AdvantagesLimitations
Quick, cheap, and easy to repeat frequentlyParticipants may not match your real target audience
Great for early, directional feedbackNot suitable for statistically rigorous conclusions
U0001F4A1 INDUSTRY INSIGHTChoosing a MethodMost professional UX teams don't rely on a single method — they mix moderated sessions for depth, unmoderated or A/B tests for scale, and guerrilla testing for fast, early direction.

Module 3 — Observing User Behavior

What users say and what users do are often two different things. A participant might say a flow “felt fine” while their mouse hovered uncertainly over three different buttons before clicking the right one. This is why observation — not just asking questions — is at the heart of usability testing.

The Think-Aloud Method

Participants are asked to verbalize their thoughts as they navigate: what they expect to happen, what confuses them, and why they click where they click. This turns an otherwise silent, invisible thought process into observable data.

What to Observe

User reactions — facial expressions, sighs, pauses

Body language — leaning in with interest vs. leaning back in frustration

Mouse movement — hovering, backtracking, erratic movement

Click patterns — repeated clicks on non-interactive elements

Scrolling behavior — excessive scrolling, missed content

Hesitation — pausing before acting, signaling uncertainty

Confusion — verbal cues like “wait, where do I…”

Task completion — whether the goal was reached, and how directly

💡 ✨ PRO TIPWatch First, Ask LaterResist the urge to jump in and explain. Let the silence sit — a confused pause is often the most valuable moment in the whole session.

Visualizing Behavior at Scale

When testing with many users — particularly in unmoderated or analytics-driven testing — teams rely on aggregated visualizations rather than watching every session individually.

Figure 3.1 — A click heatmap shows where on a page users interact most, revealing whether attention matches design intent.

Figure 3.2 — Click maps show exact interaction points; scroll maps show how far down a page users typically reach.

Session recordings capture an individual user's full journey — mouse movement, clicks, and scrolling — as a video replay, letting researchers review specific moments of confusion frame by frame.

Metrics to Observe

Qualitative observation should be paired with quantitative metrics to give a fuller picture of usability.

MetricMeaningWhy It Matters
Task Success Rate% of participants who completed the taskCore indicator of whether the design works
Task Completion TimeHow long a task takes to finishReveals friction and inefficiency
Error RateFrequency of mistakes made during a taskHighlights confusing or risky interactions
MisclicksClicks on non-functional or unintended elementsSignals unclear affordances
Navigation IssuesInstances of users getting lost or backtrackingPoints to unclear information architecture
User SatisfactionSelf-reported ease and enjoymentCaptures the emotional experience, not just success

User Feedback Collection

Observation should be complemented with structured feedback collected after the session.

Post-test interview — open conversation about the overall experience

Questionnaires and surveys — structured follow-up questions

Rating scales — quick quantifiable sentiment

System Usability Scale (SUS) — a standardized 10-item usability questionnaire

Net Promoter Score (NPS) — measures likelihood to recommend the product

Customer Satisfaction Score (CSAT) — measures satisfaction with a specific interaction

💡 U0001F4A1 INDUSTRY INSIGHTSUS in PracticeA System Usability Scale score above 68 is considered above average. Many mature product teams track SUS over time as a single, comparable usability benchmark across releases.

Module 4 — Identifying Usability Issues

Once a session is complete, the real work begins: turning raw observations into clear, actionable findings. This module covers how to recognize common categories of usability issues, rank their severity, and trace them back to their root cause.

Common Categories of Usability Issues

Navigation issues — users can't find where they need to go

Confusing labels — buttons or links that don't say what they do

Poor information hierarchy — important content is buried or de-emphasized

Hidden features — functionality that exists but isn't discoverable

Accessibility issues — barriers for users with disabilities

Slow interaction — lag or unresponsive elements that break flow

Form errors — unclear validation or confusing input requirements

Poor feedback — the system doesn't confirm what just happened

Complex user flows — too many steps to complete a simple task

Severity Levels

Not every issue deserves the same urgency. Rating severity helps teams prioritize what to fix first.

Figure 4.1 — A four-level severity scale used to triage usability findings.

Root Cause Analysis

A usability issue is a symptom — the real work is diagnosing what's actually causing it. Two commonly used techniques are the 5 Whys and the Fishbone (Ishikawa) Diagram.

📌

5 Whys — repeatedly ask “why” to drill past the symptom to the underlying cause

Fishbone Diagram — visually map potential causes across categories such as navigation, content, layout, and visual design

Observation notes, user quotes, and task analysis — supporting evidence that grounds the diagnosis in real behavior

Figure 4.2 — A fishbone diagram tracing possible root causes behind a single usability finding.

Recording Findings

Findings should be documented consistently so they can be prioritized, assigned, and tracked. A simple findings log typically includes the issue, what was observed, its severity, and a recommendation.

IssueObservationSeverityRecommendation
Checkout button not found4 of 5 users scrolled past the CTA without noticing itCriticalIncrease contrast; move CTA above the fold
Confusing filter labelsUsers hesitated and reread filter names before selectingMediumUse plain-language labels with example values
Slow image loadingUsers assumed the page had frozen and refreshed itHighAdd loading indicators; optimize image size
Small tap targets on mobileRepeated mis-taps on adjacent iconsMediumIncrease touch target size to at least 44x44px
⚠ COMMON MISTAKEVague Findings Slow Everyone Down“The navigation felt off” is not an actionable finding. Always pair an observation with concrete evidence (what happened, to whom, how often) and a specific recommendation.

Module 5 — Iteration & Product Improvement

What Is Design Iteration?

Design iteration is the repeated cycle of designing, testing, and refining a product based on evidence. Because user needs, behavior, and context are always evolving, iteration is never truly “done” — it is a continuous discipline, not a final phase of a project.

Figure 5.1 — The continuous iteration cycle: design, prototype, test, collect feedback, improve, and repeat.

Prioritizing Improvements

Not every finding can be fixed at once. Teams commonly use an Impact vs. Effort matrix to decide what to tackle first.

Figure 5.2 — Plotting findings by impact and effort helps teams decide what to fix first.

Quick Wins — high impact, low effort: tackle these immediately

Major Projects — high impact, high effort: plan and resource carefully

Fill-ins — low impact, low effort: address opportunistically

Low Priority — low impact, low effort: revisit only if time allows

Improving the Product

Improvements can take many forms depending on what the research uncovered:

Navigation improvements — simplifying menus and information architecture

Accessibility improvements — color contrast, screen-reader support, keyboard navigation

Visual improvements — clearer hierarchy, spacing, and typography

Performance improvements — faster load times and smoother interactions

Microinteractions — small animations that provide feedback and delight

Empty states — helpful guidance when there's no content yet

Loading states — clear indication that the system is working

Error messages — specific, actionable explanations instead of generic failures

Success messages — clear confirmation that an action worked

Figure 5.3 — A before-and-after comparison showing improved contrast, hierarchy, and CTA clarity.

Measuring Improvement

Every iteration should be validated by comparing metrics before and after the change — closing the loop back to Module 3's metrics.

Task completion rate

Error reduction

Time saved on task

User satisfaction (SUS, CSAT, or NPS)

Figure 5.4 — Comparing key metrics before and after an iteration validates whether the change actually worked.

💡 ✨ PRO TIPChange One Thing at a TimeWhen possible, isolate variables between iterations. If you change the layout, copy, and color all at once, you won't know which change actually drove the improvement.

Mini Case Study: Food Delivery App

Scenario

A food delivery app's analytics show a high cart-abandonment rate. The product team runs a moderated usability test with five participants to understand why. The task: “Add two items to your cart and complete checkout.”

Observed Behavior

4 of 5 participants scrolled past the Checkout button without noticing it

Participants said things like “wait, how do I actually pay?”

Two participants tapped the restaurant logo, mistaking it for a way to proceed

Average time to locate checkout: 38 seconds (task should take under 10)

Usability Issues Identified

IssueSeverity
Checkout CTA has low contrast and sits below the foldCritical
No persistent cart summary while browsing the menuHigh
Restaurant logo looks tappable but has no functionMedium

Recommendations

Add a sticky, high-contrast checkout bar that stays visible while scrolling

Show a persistent cart counter with running total

Remove or properly wire up the tappable-looking logo

Updated UI & Re-Testing

The team redesigned the checkout flow with a sticky, high-contrast bar showing the live cart total, then re-tested with five new participants.

Figure 7.1 — The original checkout screen (left) versus the redesigned, sticky checkout bar (right).

Results

MetricBeforeAfter
Task success rate62%94%
Average time to checkout38 seconds12 seconds
Reported confusionHighLow

This case illustrates the full loop: observe, identify, prioritize by severity, redesign, and validate with a second round of testing.

Common Usability Testing Mistakes

Even well-intentioned testing can produce misleading results if these common pitfalls aren't avoided.

MistakeWhy It's a Problem
Testing only with friends or colleaguesThey're too familiar with the product to react like a real first-time user
Leading the user (“don't you think this button is clear?”)Introduces bias and invalidates the observation
Interrupting participants mid-taskBreaks their natural thought process and hides real confusion
Ignoring user behavior in favor of what they sayBehavior is often more honest than self-reported opinions
Testing too few scenariosMisses edge cases and real-world variation in how the product is used
Skipping accessibility considerationsExcludes a significant portion of real users from your research
Not documenting findingsInsights get lost and can't be prioritized, tracked, or acted on
Making assumptions instead of testingReplaces evidence with guesswork, defeating the purpose of testing
⚠ COMMON MISTAKEThe Friends-and-Family TrapIt's tempting to test with people you already know because it's fast and free — but familiarity with you or the product removes the very confusion you're trying to uncover.

Best Practices Checklist

Use this checklist before, during, and after every usability testing session.

✓ Test with real users who reflect your actual target audience

✓ Observe before asking questions — let behavior speak first

✓ Focus on user goals, not just interface details

✓ Record sessions (with consent) for later review and sharing

✓ Prioritize issues by severity and impact before acting

✓ Iterate continuously rather than treating testing as a one-time event

✓ Validate improvements with a second round of testing

✓ Test accessibility alongside general usability

✓ Measure results with clear, comparable metrics

Practical Exercise

Problem Statement

Working individually or in pairs, fellows will review a mobile application of their choice, observe a real (or simulated) user's interaction with it, and produce a set of usability findings and a redesign.

Tasks

Select a mobile application and define one realistic task for a test participant to complete (e.g., “find and book an appointment”).

Observe a user attempting the task — a classmate, friend, or simulated first-time use of your own.

Identify at least five usability issues based on your observation.

Assign a severity level (Critical, High, Medium, or Low) to each issue.

Suggest a specific, actionable improvement for each issue.

Redesign one screen based on your findings, and be ready to explain your reasoning.

Deliverables

A findings log (Issue / Observation / Severity / Recommendation)

One redesigned screen (sketch, wireframe, or mockup)

A two-minute walkthrough explaining what changed and why

Evaluation Rubric

CriteriaWhat Reviewers Look For
Quality of ObservationFindings are specific, evidence-based, and clearly documented
Severity JudgmentSeverity levels are reasonable and justified
ActionabilityRecommendations are concrete and directly address the issue
Redesign QualityThe redesign visibly addresses the highest-severity finding
CommunicationThe walkthrough clearly connects findings to design decisions
