01 Introduction to Design Methods

Design methods are structured thinking tools that guide how a team understands problems and builds solutions. They are not rigid rules — you select the right method for the stage you are in, just as a craftsperson picks the right tool for the job.

What Design Methods Cover

Discover methods — interviews, field studies, contextual inquiry, analytics review

Define methods — affinity mapping, personas, journey maps, HMW statements

Develop methods — brainstorming, Crazy 8s, sketching, design studios, storyboarding

Deliver methods — usability testing, A/B testing, heuristic evaluation, accessibility audits

Design methods mapped across four phases

💡 Key TakeawaysMethods don’t create solutions — they reveal the right problem

02 Why Structured Design Matters in UX

Without structure, design becomes opinion-driven — whoever speaks loudest in the room wins. Structured design creates a repeatable, defensible process where every decision traces back to user evidence.

What Unstructured Design Looks Like

Someone has an idea → team jumps straight to design without research

Build it → ship a "clean" UI that users still find confusing

Expensive rework → fix it post-launch at 10× the cost

What Structured Design Produces

Early risk reduction — wrong assumptions caught before engineering begins

Stakeholder alignment — everyone sees the "why" behind every design decision

Measurable outcomes — decisions are tied to metrics, not aesthetics

Faster delivery — fewer late-stage revisions because problems were solved earlier

💡 Key TakeawaysMost design failures are not bad UI — they are wrong assumptions

03 Evolution of Design — UI → UX → Experience Design

Design has grown from making things look good (UI), to making them work well (UX), to making entire experiences feel meaningful (Experience Design). Each era builds on — not replaces — the previous.

Three Eras of Design Maturity

UI Design (1990s–2000s) — Visual aesthetics, typography, color, layout, screen composition

UX Design (2005–2015) — User research, information architecture, interaction design, usability

Experience Design (2015–present) — Service design, systems thinking, emotional journeys, ethics, inclusion

Design evolution timeline: additive, not replacement

💡 Key TakeawaysUI without UX creates beautiful but confusing products — form without function.UX without Experience Design creates functional but forgettable products — it works but doesn't connect.Today's best products operate at all three layers simultaneously. Dropping any layer creates gaps users notice.

04 Design Thinking Process

Design Thinking is a human-centered problem-solving framework. It separates problem understanding from solution building — preventing teams from rushing to "the answer" before they have truly understood "the question."

The Five Stages

Empathize — Observe and interview real users. Remove assumptions. See through their eyes.

Define — Synthesise research into a clear, actionable problem statement. Use HMW questions.

Ideate — Generate without judgment. Run Crazy 8s, brainstorms, design studios. Volume first.

Prototype — Build low-fidelity sketches or clickable mocks to externalise ideas cheaply.

Test — Observe real users with your prototype. Listen for confusion and workarounds.

Design Thinking iterative loop (stages can loop back as you learn)

💡 Key TakeawaysDesign Thinking is iterative — you can loop from Test back to Empathize when you find your problem framing was wrong.The key power is separating "understanding the problem" from "solving it" — preventing premature solutions.Empathy is not a soft skill — it is the foundation of every defensible design decision.

05 Double Diamond Method

Created by the British Design Council in 2005, the Double Diamond visualises design as two connected diamonds. The first explores the problem space (finding the right problem). The second explores the solution space (finding the right solution).

Structure of the Double Diamond

Diamond 1 — Problem Space

— Diverge. Conduct broad research to understand context, users, and market.

— Converge. Synthesise findings into a clear, focused problem statement.

Diamond 2 — Solution Space

— Diverge. Generate many possible solutions through ideation and prototyping.

— Converge. Test, validate, and refine down to the right solution to ship.

Double Diamond: two diamonds, four phases, one continuous flow

💡 Key TakeawaysThe gap between the two diamonds is intentional — it is where you decide what problem is worth solving.Most teams skip Diamond 1 entirely and end up solving the wrong problem brilliantly.Both diamonds require both divergence and convergence — exploration followed by decision.

06 Divergence vs Convergence

Every creative process oscillates between two modes: diverge (open up, explore, generate) and converge (close down, evaluate, decide). Knowing when to be in each mode — and protecting both — is a core design skill.

Diverge Mode

Suspend judgment — no idea is too wild; evaluation kills creative momentum prematurely

Maximise quantity — volume of ideas increases the chance of finding an unexpected breakthrough

Include everyone — different perspectives surface assumptions that internal teams share invisibly

Converge Mode

Apply criteria — score options against user needs, business goals, and technical feasibility

Commit to one path — indecision is a decision to do nothing; convergence enables progress

Document the reasoning — record why ideas were dropped; useful when stakeholders revisit discarded concepts

Diverge expands possibilities; Converge narrows to the best choice

💡 Key TakeawaysDiverge and converge are distinct mindsets — mixing them kills both. Run them as separate sessions.Teams that converge too early miss breakthrough solutions that only emerge after the obvious ideas run out.Teams that never converge ship nothing. Both modes are essential; neither is optional.

07 Mapping — Design Thinking vs Double Diamond

Both frameworks share DNA but organise it differently. Design Thinking is stage-based and practitioner-focused — it is what you run in the room. Double Diamond is phase-based and organisationally legible — it is what you show stakeholders.

How They Align

Empathize maps to Discover — both are about broadening understanding through user research

Define maps to Define — both are about synthesising insight into a sharp problem statement

Ideate maps to Develop — both involve diverging into possible solutions

Prototype bridges Develop and Deliver — it is the artefact that connects ideation to validation

Test maps to Deliver — both converge on the right solution through validation

Design Thinking stages mapped to Double Diamond phases

💡 Key TakeawaysUse Design Thinking with your team in daily sprints — it is a practitioner's operational tool.Use Double Diamond to brief leadership and plan quarterly roadmaps — it speaks in phases, not stages.The Define stage appears in both frameworks. This is intentional — it is the most critical and most commonly skipped step in all design processes.

08 End-to-End UX Lifecycle

The UX Lifecycle is the full journey of a product from idea to continuous improvement. It is not a one-time process — it is a living system. Every launch is the beginning of the next research cycle.

Nine Phases of the Lifecycle

Discovery — User research, stakeholder interviews, competitive landscape analysis

Strategy — Define goals and KPIs, create personas, write HMW problem statements

Information Architecture — Sitemap, content hierarchy, user flows, navigation structure

Interaction Design — Wireframes, interaction patterns, micro-interactions, state design

Visual Design — UI design, design system, component library, accessibility compliance

Prototyping — Lo-fi sketches through hi-fi clickable Figma prototypes

Usability Testing — Moderated sessions, A/B tests, tree testing, card sorting

Launch — Staged rollout, engineering handoff, QA, accessibility audit

Continuous Improvement — Analytics monitoring, feedback loops, next research cycle begins

End-to-end UX lifecycle with continuous feedback loop back to Discovery

💡 Key TakeawaysThe lifecycle is circular — post-launch data feeds the next discovery cycle. There is no finish line.Skipping phases does not save time; it shifts costs to rework later at 5–10× the expense.The handoff between Design and Engineering is where value most often gets lost — invest in thorough specification and shared language.

09 Real-World Case Study — Food Delivery Reorder Flow

A food delivery app (Swiggy / Zomato style) noticed that 60% of users who completed an order never reordered from the same restaurant. The team suspected the reorder experience was broken. Here is the full UX process applied end-to-end.

A food delivery app (Swiggy / Zomato style) noticed that 60% of users who completed an order never reordered from the same restaurant. The team suspected the reorder experience was broken. Here is the full UX process applied end-to-end.

Business Context

Trigger metric — 60% of first-time orderers did not reorder within 30 days

Initial assumption — users disliked the restaurant quality (wrong)

Actual problem — discovered through research to be a navigation friction issue

Case study step flow: Problem → Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test → Iterate → Launch → Outcome

Process Walkthrough

Step 1: Empathize

We started by understanding real user behavior through 12 in-depth interviews and 40 session recording reviews.

One pattern stood out immediately: instead of going to Order History, users were repeatedly navigating to Search just to find restaurants they had already ordered from. This wasn’t a one-off—it was consistent behavior, pointing to a deeper discoverability issue.

Step 2 : Define

We synthesized over 180 research notes using affinity mapping, which revealed three clear problem areas:

The reorder flow was buried (taking 4+ taps)

Order history was difficult to scan

Lack of food thumbnails made recognition harder

This led to a focused problem statement: How might we make reordering as fast and effortless as a single tap?

Step 3 : Ideate

To explore solutions, we ran two Crazy 8s sessions and generated 24 concepts in under 90 minutes.

Each idea was evaluated using a simple scoring framework:

Speed to reorder

Engineering effort

User familiarity

From this, we narrowed it down to three strong directions to prototype.

Step 4 : Prototype

We built three high-fidelity, test-ready concepts in Figma within three days:

A reorder card directly on the homepage

A redesigned, more scannable order history

A smart reorder notification

All concepts were interactive and ready for usability testing.

Step 5: Test

We conducted 8 moderated remote sessions to evaluate performance:

Homepage reorder card: ~12 seconds

Redesigned history: ~34 seconds

Smart notification: ~8 seconds (but depended on opt-in)

While the notification was fastest, it relied on external triggers. The homepage reorder card struck the best balance between speed, reliability, and user trust—making it the clear winner.

Step 6 : Iterate

Testing revealed two key friction points:

Food thumbnails were too small for quick recognition

Pricing wasn’t visible before interaction

After refining these, we ran a quick validation with 5 users. All users completed the task in under 10 seconds—with zero confusion.

Step 7 : Launch

We rolled out the feature to 10% of users and tracked key metrics:

Reorder rate

Time to reorder

Session length

Within the first week, all indicators showed positive movement, leading to a full rollout approval.

💡 Key TakeawaysThe right solution (one-tap reorder card) was not the obvious solution (redesign the restaurant page).Only structured research revealed the true navigation friction. Assumptions would have led to a wrong fix.Every step produced a decision-quality artefact: insights, problem statement, scored concepts, tested prototype, measured outcome.The team's willingness to reframe the problem — not just the UI — was the actual design skill on display.
